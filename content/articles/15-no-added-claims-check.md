# No Added Supplement Claims

No added supplement claims can help Singapore buyers notice cleaner label wording, but they should not be treated as proof that a product is safer, more natural, or suitable for every person. This guide is educational information only, not medical advice, and it shows how to read "no added" wording beside the full ingredient list and warnings.

## What no added wording can mean

"No added" usually points to something a product says it does not include, such as a colour, sweetener, allergen, preservative, or flavour. That can be useful when you are comparing a clean-label supplement in Singapore.

The limit is important. A no-added claim is not the same as a complete ingredient review. It does not automatically explain the active ingredient form, capsule shell, excipients, allergen traces, country of manufacture, warning statements, or whether the product claim stays within Singapore health supplement rules. Treat the phrase as a starting label cue, not the final buying decision.

## Two live label examples

Captured on 4 July 2026, Blackmores Singapore's Omega Triple page listed "No added salt, yeast, gluten, wheat, milk derivatives or preservatives" and "No added artificial colours or sweeteners." The same visible product copy also said it contains fish, sulfites, and soya bean products. That is a useful original label-reading example: a no-added list can sit beside a contains list, and both matter.

Captured on the same date, Swisse Singapore's Men's Ultivite 50+ page listed "No added lactose, gluten, yeast, egg or artificial flavours" while also saying the product contains soya beans and is not vegan or vegetarian friendly because it contains gelatin from a vitamin A raw material. The ingredient section also named bulking agents, coating ingredients, anti-caking agents, colours, and other components. The lesson is not that either product is good or bad; it is that "no added" wording must be read with the whole label.

| Label cue | What it helps with | What still needs checking |
|---|---|---|
| No added gluten or lactose | Screens one avoidance need | Other allergens, traces, capsule shell, and suitability warnings |
| No added artificial colours | Shows one colour-related position | Natural colours, coating agents, active ingredients, and claim wording |
| No added preservatives | Flags one additive category | Storage instructions, expiry date, batch details, and opened-product handling |
| Contains fish, soya, or gelatin | Identifies a specific inclusion | Personal allergies, dietary restrictions, and whether alternatives are needed |

## Match it to HSA claim rules

HSA's health supplement claims page says health supplement claims may support or maintain health, well-being, or physiological processes, but health supplements must not be labelled, advertised, or promoted for a specific medicinal purpose. The same page warns against disease-style treatment or prevention claims.

For no-added wording, the practical check is whether the page quietly moves from ingredient absence into broad safety or disease language. "No added artificial flavours" is a narrow label statement. "No artificial flavours, therefore safe for everyone" would be a much stronger claim and should not be accepted without evidence. HSA's claims substantiation guideline also says product materials should not imply no adverse effects, complete safety, or that safety results from natural origin.

## Use a four-pass clean-label check

1. Read the exact no-added phrase and identify the ingredient category it covers.
2. Read the contains, warning, allergen, and suitability lines before deciding whether the phrase matters to you.
3. Check the full active and inactive ingredient list, including capsule shell, coating, colours, sweeteners, preservatives, and anti-caking agents.
4. Compare the marketing claim with HSA-style support wording and avoid disease, prevention, promised-outcome, or natural-origin safety language.

This process is slower than reacting to the front label, but it prevents a common clean-label mistake: overvaluing the missing ingredient while ignoring the ingredients that are actually present.

## What natural does not settle

HSA's health supplement overview recognises that health supplements may include vitamins, minerals, amino acids, fatty acids, enzymes, probiotics, other bioactive substances, substances derived from animal, mineral, or botanical sources, and synthetic sources of those ingredients. That means "natural" and "synthetic" are not enough by themselves to judge a product.

A natural-positioned supplement can still contain allergens, animal-derived materials, excipients, warning statements, or claims that need careful reading. A product with synthetic-source ingredients is not automatically unsuitable either. The safer clean-label habit is to ask: what exactly is included, what exactly is excluded, what does the warning say, and what claim is being made?

## FAQ

**Does "no added" mean a supplement is allergen-free?**  
No. It only covers the ingredient category named in the phrase. Always read the contains, allergen, and warning lines.

**Is a natural supplement safer if it has no artificial colours?**  
Not automatically. HSA guidance warns against implying that safety comes from being natural, and suitability depends on the full ingredient list and personal circumstances.

**Should I avoid every bulking agent or coating ingredient?**  
Not as a blanket rule. Use the ingredient list to understand what is present, then check whether any item conflicts with your dietary needs, allergies, or professional advice.
