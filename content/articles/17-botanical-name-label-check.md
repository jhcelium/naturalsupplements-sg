*Written by the Natural Supplements Singapore editorial team · Last updated: 14 July 2026*

A botanical name label check helps Singapore supplement buyers separate a precise plant-ingredient record from a broad front-label phrase such as “herbal” or “natural”. This educational guide, not medical advice, shows how to read the scientific name, plant part, preparation, and quantity without treating those details as proof of effectiveness or personal suitability.

## Why botanical names matter

The same common plant name can cover different species, and different parts of one plant can be prepared in different ways. A label that only says “valerian”, for example, gives less information than one that identifies *Valeriana officinalis*, the root, an extract form, and an amount.

The [HSA labelling standards](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-chp-labelling-guidelines.pdf) give a useful official reading rule. For an active ingredient derived from a plant or animal, the name and quantity should be declared using the scientific name, followed by the plant or animal part and the preparation type where applicable; a common or local name is optional. This rule set was captured on 14 July 2026. It is a label-transparency check, not a quality score.

## Build a four-field reading record

Use four fields before comparing any botanical supplement. This structure is the first original element in this guide, derived from the live HSA labelling standard and captured on 14 July 2026.

| Field | What to record | Why it prevents a shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Scientific name | The two-part botanical name, such as *Valeriana officinalis* | Avoids relying on a broad common name alone |
| Plant part | Root, flower, fruiting body, leaf, seed, or another named part | Shows which part supplies the labelled ingredient |
| Preparation | Dry extract, concentrate, powder, oil, or another stated form | Separates the raw plant name from the prepared ingredient |
| Quantity | Amount per dosage form or recommended dose, plus an equivalent amount if shown | Makes two labels easier to compare without assuming equal strength |

HSA’s January 2026 [Health Supplements Guidelines](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-hs-guidelines.pdf) also recognise botanical materials in forms such as extracts, isolates, and concentrates. The guidelines say dealers should hold product information including the manufacturer and country of manufacture for each ingredient, but this does not mean every consumer-facing product page will display that supplier-level record. Do not fill a missing field with a guess.

## Two live Singapore label examples

Two official Singapore brand pages illustrate how the four fields appear in practice. Captured on 14 July 2026, [Blackmores Singapore’s Valerian Forte page](https://www.blackmores.com.sg/products/valerian-forte) listed *Valeriana officinalis*, extract dry concentrate 400 mg, equivalent to dry root 2 g, standardised to contain valerenic acids 3.2 mg per tablet.

On the same date, [Swisse Singapore’s Ultiboost Sleep page](https://swisse.com.sg/products/swisse-ultiboost-sleep) named five botanical records in its equivalent-extract line: *Anemarrhena asphodeloides* root, *Humulus lupulus* flower, *Poria cocos* fruiting body, *Glycyrrhiza glabra* root and stolon, and *Valeriana officinalis* root. The page also gave a dry-equivalent amount for each.

| Live page | Scientific-name coverage | Part and preparation clues | Quantity clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blackmores Valerian Forte | One named botanical | Root and rhizome; dry extract concentrate | Extract amount, dry-root equivalent, and standardised constituent |
| Swisse Ultiboost Sleep | Five named botanicals | Root, flower, fruiting body, and root/stolon; dry extract concentrates | A dry-equivalent amount for each botanical |

This comparison is the second original element. It records how two live labels organise botanical identity; it does not compare benefits, recommend either product, or establish that one formula is better.

## Use a six-step label checklist

1. Find the scientific name rather than stopping at the common plant name.
2. Record the plant part exactly as shown; do not assume root, leaf, seed, or fruit are interchangeable.
3. Identify the preparation, such as powder, oil, or dry extract concentrate.
4. Match the quantity to the correct unit: per tablet, capsule, serving, or recommended dose.
5. Keep the extract amount, raw-herb equivalent, and standardised constituent in separate columns if the label gives all three.
6. Mark absent information as “not shown on the checked page” and ask the seller or brand for the current label rather than inventing a value.

The checklist is especially useful when a product page uses a short marketing phrase but a longer ingredient panel. Read the ingredient panel, warning text, directions, and package image together. A complete botanical name does not cancel warnings or make a product suitable for every person.

## FAQ

**Is the common plant name enough?**  
It can help with orientation, but the scientific name is more precise. HSA’s labelling standard makes the common or local name optional while recommending internationally accepted ingredient nomenclature.

**Does a larger raw-herb equivalent prove a better supplement?**  
No. The number describes the label record; it does not by itself prove quality, effectiveness, absorption, or suitability. Compare like with like and avoid turning one quantity into a medical conclusion.

**Are extract amount and herb-equivalent amount the same?**  
Not necessarily. A concentrated extract may be listed with its own amount and an equivalent amount of dry plant material. Keep both values and their units instead of merging them.

**What if the product page omits the plant part or preparation?**  
Record the omission. Check the current package label or ask the seller or manufacturer for a clearer ingredient record before relying on the page.

## Keep the label check within its limits

A botanical name label check supports transparent comparison, but it cannot diagnose a condition, select a dose, predict an interaction, or establish that a supplement will work. This article is general consumer education, not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, or a recommendation to use or stop a product. If you are pregnant, nursing, taking medicines, managing a health condition, buying for a child, or concerned about a reaction, speak with a qualified doctor or pharmacist.
